About this model

What this shows

This is the one place in the solar system where the torus is literal. The Sun's magnetic field, below the surface, is wound into two rings that encircle the rotation axis — one per hemisphere, carrying opposite polarity. The near hemisphere of the Sun is cut away so you can see them.

The winding is the thing to watch. Turn on Ω-effect winding and you see meridional field lines — lines running north to south — being dragged by differential rotation. The Sun turns faster at its equator than near its poles, so a line threaded through that shear gets stretched east-west until it closes on itself as a hoop. That is the Ω-effect, and the hoops it makes are the belts.

Everything runs on the eleven-year cycle. The belts build, migrate toward the equator, throw up loops that surface as sunspot pairs, and dissolve; then the field rebuilds with the polarities swapped. Two of those cycles — twenty-two years — make one full round trip, the Hale cycle.

Why the spots come in pairs

Magnetic pressure makes a flux tube lighter than the plasma around it, so sections of the belt bow upward. Where a loop breaks the photosphere it leaves two dark marks — one where the field exits, one where it re-enters. That is why sunspots come in pairs, and why the leading spot in the northern hemisphere has the opposite polarity to the leading spot in the south.

The polarity orientation flips between hemispheres and flips again with each new cycle. That regularity is Hale's law, and it is the strongest observational evidence that the thing underneath is a ring rather than a tangle.

The two geometries

The Sun alternates between a poloidal field, running pole to pole like a bar magnet, and a toroidal field, the belts. Poloidal is what you see at solar minimum; toroidal is what produces the spots at maximum. Turn both overlays on and watch the cycle — one fades as the other builds.

What the solar wind carries out to the heliopause is the toroidal phase, unwound and stretched across the solar system. Turn on Parker spiral to see that handoff: the rotation of the footpoints inside a radially streaming wind draws the field into a spiral that is nearly azimuthal by the time it reaches the outer planets.

How deep are the belts?

This is live and contested, so the model lets you move them.

The textbook position is the tachocline at about 0.7 solar radii — the thin shear layer where the rigidly rotating radiative interior meets the differentially rotating convection zone. That is the default here.

Vasil and colleagues argued in 2024 that helioseismology puts low-latitude torsional oscillations in the outer five to ten percent of the Sun — the near-surface shear layer — and that a magneto-rotational instability there better explains what is observed, with belt strengths of only 300 to 1,000 gauss. A January 2026 helioseismic analysis pushed back, finding butterfly-like behavior in the rotation gradient near the tachocline, and read that as favoring a deep-seated dynamo.

Unsettled, and being argued with data. The depth buttons let you see what each reading looks like.

Where the model simplifies

We cannot measure subsurface magnetic fields by helioseismology. Depth, strength and shape of the belts are all inferred from what surfaces and from flow patterns — so the tidy rings drawn here are a schematic of an inference, not a picture of a measurement.

Real emergence happens at many points along a belt at once, not from a handful of loops. The belts themselves are almost certainly fibril and ragged rather than smooth tubes. Differential rotation varies with depth as well as latitude, and only the latitude part is drawn. The winding here completes in one cycle for legibility; the real shear rate and the real field strengths are not to scale with each other.

The Sun's true color is white — the warm cream here follows how the photosphere is conventionally rendered, and how it looks through an atmosphere.

On the Ra material

I have left the Ra correspondences out of this one. The 75,000-year structure in the Ra material resolves to precession and its twelfths, which is an Earth-axis cycle — it has no counterpart in the solar dynamo, whose longest detectable rhythms run to a couple of thousand years at most. Drawing a correspondence here would be inventing one.

My proposal: the honest bridge between this material and the archaeoastronomy interest runs through cosmic-ray shielding, not through solar periodicity. The heliosphere blocks roughly seventy percent of galactic cosmic rays, and passages through denser interstellar clouds compress it and thin that shielding. That is a real mechanism on a tens-of-thousands-of-years timescale. It is a separate build.

I'm offering these thoughts for what they are worth.
